# Denver Has 26,941 Active Long-Term Rental Licenses, Held by 20,780 Holders

Denver's long-term rental register holds 38,970 rows, and the first fact about it is that a row is not a license. Rows sharing a license number are the unit and address filings of a single license: one license in this register carries 697 rows, one for each unit address of one apartment property on one parcel, while other licenses for whole multi-unit buildings carry a single row. Counted by distinct license number, the register pulled on October 20, 2026 holds 26,941 licenses with an active status, under 20,780 holder names as filed. On that license basis, 68.6 percent of active licenses are their holder's only license (18,471 of 26,939 named active licenses); the largest holder accounts for 525 licenses (1.95 percent), the top 10 holders together for 4.86 percent, and the 65 holders with 11 or more licenses for 8.39 percent. By holder name classification, 37.1 percent of active licenses sit under a name carrying a business-entity marker and 59.4 percent under an individual name; that is a classification of the name as filed, not of beneficial ownership. On parcels, rows stack where one license enumerates a building's units: 2,846 parcels carry two or more rows, and the largest parcel carries 697 rows under a single license, against 229 units on its assessor record. By neighborhood, the licensed footprint appears in all 78 statistical neighborhoods, at a citywide rate of 108.4 active rows per 1,000 housing units against the short-term rental register's 5.8 active licenses per 1,000 units in the companion short-term rental brief (October 19, 2026); the two registers use different grains, and every comparison in this brief keeps their labels attached.

All figures below come from the residential rental property license register published by the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection (DLCP) in the Denver Open Data Catalog, pulled in full on October 20, 2026: 38,970 rows. A record is counted as active in this brief only when its status reads exactly "License Issued - Active" (38,949 rows, 26,941 distinct license numbers). The same table carries 21 Delinquent rows under 21 further license numbers; those are counted separately wherever they appear and never merged into "active." This brief reports the licensed footprint only. Rentals operating without a license are not in this register and cannot be counted from it, and the register's row grain (Finding 1) means its rows are not a count of rental units either; the unit coverage the data can support is stated in Finding 5 from assessor records, with its limits attached.

## Finding 1: One row is not one license, and neither count is a unit count: 38,970 rows, 26,962 license numbers

The register's grain is the finding everything else depends on, so it is printed first. The 38,970 rows carry 26,962 distinct license numbers (26,941 active; 21 delinquent, with no license number mixing statuses). 24,129 license numbers carry exactly one row. 2,833 license numbers carry two or more rows, holding 14,841 rows between them (38.1 percent of the register); the largest carries 697 rows. A row-level check of every license with 100 or more rows (12 licenses) confirms the pattern with one documented exception: 11 of the 12 sit under one holder name, on one parcel, with a distinct address on every row - the rows enumerate the units of one property. The 12th files each of its 86 unit addresses twice, once under each of two parcel numbers (172 rows in total), a cross-parcel double filing counted as published and taken up again in Finding 5. Only 19 active licenses span more than one parcel, and only 8 span more than one neighborhood, which fits the program's own structure: the DLCP residential rental FAQ states that each single-unit property requires an individual license, while a multi-unit property can hold a single license covering multiple units, addresses, or structures on a single parcel or contiguous parcels under the same ownership (Sources).

The grain runs in both directions, and the second direction is the one a row count would hide. Bridged to the assessor parcel file (38,427 of 38,949 active rows matched, 98.7 percent; the bridge is described in Methods) and compared against each parcel's assessor unit count (TOT_UNITS on the parcel record), the 26,309 matched licensed parcels with a unit count recorded divide as follows: on 22,107 parcels the row count equals the parcel record's unit count; on 2,745 parcels rows run below it; on 1,457 parcels rows run above it. The below cases concentrate in one shape: 23,548 licensed parcels carry a single row, and 1,026 of those single-row parcels carry 10 or more units on their assessor record (73,501 units in total) - a whole building filed as one row. The above cases are the unit-enumerated licenses, whose row counts can exceed the parcel record's own unit figure (the largest parcel: 697 rows against 229 units on the record). A built-in control at the register's single-unit end behaves as expected: among single-row licenses with a parcel unit record, 89.2 percent sit on parcels whose record carries exactly 1 unit.

The practical consequence, applied throughout this brief: holder concentration is computed on distinct license numbers (the license basis), footprint rates are computed on rows and labeled row rates (the row basis), and unit coverage is computed from assessor records (Finding 5). No figure in this brief adds or converts between the three.

The program context explains the register's shape over time. Under the city's residential rental licensing program (Denver Revised Municipal Code chapter 32, article III, as described on DLCP's program pages), a license is required for anyone offering, providing, or operating a residential rental property for 30 days or more; the requirement took effect for multi-unit properties on January 1, 2023 and for single-unit properties on January 1, 2024, with applications accepted from March 2022, and a license is valid for four years unless ownership changes (Sources). The register's coverage is therefore defined by that phase-in: by October 2026 every long-term rental property in Denver has been inside the requirement for at least 33 months. What the register cannot show is how many rental properties remain outside it; that count would need a denominator this package does not have (Finding 5).

## Finding 2: On the license basis, 68.6 percent of active licenses are their holder's only license, and the largest holder holds 525

Counted on the holder name as filed (normalized for case and spacing; the counting rule is in Methods), the 26,939 active licenses that carry a holder name sit under 20,780 distinct names; 2 active licenses, on 2 rows, carry no holder name at all and are excluded from holder counts only. The distribution, by licenses held per holder: 1 license, 18,471 holders; 2 licenses, 1,430 holders; 3 to 5 licenses, 685 holders; 6 to 10, 129 holders; 11 or more, 65 holders. Holders with exactly one license hold 18,471 licenses (68.6 percent). Holders with two or more hold 8,468 licenses (31.4 percent), and the 65 holders with 11 or more hold 2,259 licenses (8.4 percent). The largest single holder accounts for 525 of 26,939 named active licenses (1.95 percent). The top 5 holders together account for 1,048 licenses (3.89 percent), the top 10 for 1,310 (4.86 percent), and the top 25 for 1,712 (6.36 percent). On the full register, every status included, the same computation gives 26,962 licenses and the same ordering at the top.

The same holder tops the row basis, which measures something different and is printed separately for that reason: the largest holder's 525 licenses carry 850 rows (2.18 percent of the 38,947 active rows that carry a holder name). Across all holders, the 260 holders with 11 or more rows on the row basis hold 10,322 rows (26.5 percent), against 8.4 percent of licenses held by the 65 holders in the 11-or-more license band. The gap between the two percentages is the grain of Finding 1, not a second concentration finding: a holder whose licenses enumerate every unit of a large building accumulates rows faster than licenses, and a holder whose buildings are filed as single rows accumulates neither. Row-basis concentration overstates license concentration for unit-enumerating filers and understates unit exposure for single-row filers; neither basis measures units held, which is why Finding 5 reports unit coverage from assessor records instead.

Name-variant sensitivity cuts differently here than it did in the short-term rental register, and the difference is printed rather than smoothed. Re-counting holders with names merged on first and last token only leaves the largest holder unchanged at 525 licenses, but reduces the group count from 20,780 to 17,496 and puts 45.4 percent of licenses in groups of two or more, because business names share first and last tokens across distinct entities (two unrelated companies can share a first word and a closing LLC token). An over-merge on first initial and surname or final token, which certainly combines different holders, produces a maximum group of 1,160 licenses and puts 60.8 percent of licenses in groups of two or more; it is printed as an upper bound on grouping by name tokens, not as a measurement. The register records the holder name as filed and no ownership identifier, so the exact-name count is the reproducible basis and the merges mark how far token grouping alone could move it. Common ownership behind distinct filed names (a parent company behind separate LLCs, properties held through managers) cannot be seen in this field, and this brief does not estimate it.

The name classification is the mirror image of the short-term register's. Every active license was classified by the same documented token rule used in the October 19, 2026 short-term rental brief (Methods): a name is a business-entity name if any token is a business marker (LLC, INC, LTD, CORP, LLP, TRUST, COMPANY, and the other tokens listed in the build code), an individual name if it is two to five alphabetic tokens with no business marker, and not determined otherwise. On all 26,941 active licenses the result is: individual names 16,012 (59.4 percent), business-entity names 9,994 (37.1 percent), not determined 935 (3.5 percent). On the row basis the business share rises to 49.4 percent (19,230 of 38,949 rows), because business-named holders operate the unit-enumerated multi-unit licenses; on holders it falls to 31.1 percent (6,469 of 20,780). This is a classification of the name as filed, not of beneficial ownership: an individual can hold property through an entity the register does not name, and an entity name on a license does not by itself establish how many units sit behind it.

## Finding 3: Rows stack on parcels under single licenses: 2,846 parcels carry two or more rows, and 205 parcels carry two or more licenses

Bridged to the assessor parcel file, the 38,427 matched active rows sit on 26,394 distinct parcels. On 23,548 of those parcels there is exactly one active row. On 2,846 parcels there are two or more rows, holding 14,879 rows between them (38.7 percent of matched rows). The largest count is 697 rows on one parcel in the North Capitol Hill neighborhood, under a single license, against 229 units on that parcel's assessor record; the second largest is 444 rows on one parcel in Central Park under 5 licenses, against 398 units on its record. Parcel row counts in this register measure filing grain and building size together, and the two examples show both: the counts are printed as rows, with the parcel record's unit figure beside them, never as units licensed.

Counted by distinct license numbers instead, parcel stacking nearly disappears: 205 parcels carry two or more distinct active licenses, holding 559 licenses between them, and the maximum is 29 distinct licenses on one parcel in the Hampden South neighborhood, whose assessor record carries 8 units - the condominium pattern, in which separately owned units on a shared parcel license individually. The two parcel statements are both true and answer different questions: buildings concentrate rows; ownership parcels rarely concentrate licenses. A reader who counted rows per parcel and reported the result as licenses per operator would overstate holder concentration by exactly the mechanism in Finding 1; a reader who counted licenses per parcel would miss building scale entirely. This brief prints the two separately throughout.

## Finding 4: The footprint covers all 78 neighborhoods; the densest long-term row rate is 2.6 times the citywide rate, and the two rental registers rank neighborhoods similarly

Matched to parcels and through them to the city's 78 statistical neighborhoods, 38,427 of 38,949 active rows (98.7 percent) carry a neighborhood; the 522 unassigned rows are the rows the parcel bridge could not place in a neighborhood, concentrated on a small number of parcels: 499 of them sit on parcels absent from the parcel spine, and 23 sit on 22 parcels that are present in the spine but carry no neighborhood value (counted by parcel number alone, the bridge matches 38,450 active rows); the two largest unassigned parcels hold 157 of the 522 rows. Active rows appear in all 78 neighborhoods; this register has no zero neighborhoods. By count, the largest footprints are Capitol Hill (1,887 active rows), Central Park (1,617), Five Points (1,503), Speer (1,321), and Gateway - Green Valley Ranch (1,227). The five largest together hold 19.7 percent of assigned rows; the ten largest hold 32.7 percent. By distinct licenses, the largest counts are Gateway - Green Valley Ranch (1,198 licenses), Capitol Hill (1,008), Central Park (855), Speer (818), and Five Points (784); Gateway - Green Valley Ranch ranks first on licenses and fifth on rows because its licenses are predominantly single-row, single-unit filings.

Counts favor big neighborhoods, so the exposure measure is rows per 1,000 housing units (ACS 2020-2024 5-year housing units allocated to the statistical neighborhoods; basis in Methods), labeled a row rate because the numerator is filings, not units. Citywide, Denver carries 108.4 active rows per 1,000 housing units (all active rows over the allocated denominator; on assigned rows only, 106.9). The median neighborhood rate is 107.1 per 1,000. The highest rates are Whittier (286.1 per 1,000 units, 644 rows), Clayton (204.3, 374 rows), City Park West (200.8, 767 rows), University (197.7, 754 rows), and Cole (194.6, 393 rows). Whittier's rate is 2.6 times the citywide rate. These are filings per housing unit, not rentals per rental unit; neighborhoods differ in tenure mix, and the warehouse holds no neighborhood-level renter-occupied denominator for this brief (Finding 5).

Against the short-term rental footprint in the companion brief (October 19, 2026), the comparison is one of scale and of similar geography, with the measures labeled because they differ. The short-term register holds 2,083 active licenses (one license per licensee, one unit per license under that program's primary-residence rules) at 5.8 licenses per 1,000 housing units citywide, present in 75 of 78 neighborhoods. The long-term register holds 26,941 active licenses, 12.9 times the short-term license count, and 38,949 active rows, 18.7 times the short-term license count, present in all 78 neighborhoods; the three neighborhoods with no short-term licenses (Auraria, Kennedy, Sun Valley) all carry long-term rows. Across the 78 neighborhoods, the rank correlation between the long-term row rate and the short-term license rate is 0.65, and between the raw counts it is 0.70. Whittier ranks first on both rate tables (286.1 long-term rows and 27.1 short-term licenses per 1,000 units) and Cole sits in the top five of both. The rankings diverge where housing form diverges: North Capitol Hill ranks seventh on the long-term row rate (183.3, driven by large multi-unit buildings) and 51st of 78 on the short-term rate (3.2 per 1,000, 19 licenses). The correlation is a geographic statement, not a causal one: it says the neighborhoods with the densest licensed long-term filings are, in rank terms, often the neighborhoods with the densest licensed short-term presence, and it does not say either produced the other. The two rates are never added in this brief: their numerators are different objects (unit filings under licenses of any size, and single-unit licenses), and a combined rate would have no unit of count.

## Finding 5: What the register can and cannot say about coverage, and the fields that disagree with themselves

The pick for this brief asked how much of Denver's long-term rental stock sits in the licensed register. The register and the warehouse can answer that question only in part, and the boundary is printed here rather than filled in. The warehouse's ACS layer carries total housing units by neighborhood (table B25001); it does not carry a renter-occupied unit count at any geography, so no licensed share of rental stock can be computed from the layers this package joins, and none is printed. Any coverage rate quoted as licenses or rows over total housing units would divide by a denominator that includes owner-occupied homes and vacant units, and this brief does not present one as a coverage figure.

What can be computed on a single consistent basis is the assessor-record comparison. The 26,309 licensed parcels with a unit count on their assessor record carry 133,839 units on those records; all parcels on the same spine carry 445,057 units on theirs, so 30.1 percent of the assessor-record unit total sits on parcels that hold at least one active long-term rental license. That figure is printed with its exact scope: it counts every unit on a licensed parcel, including any owner-occupied or non-rental units on the same parcel; it counts no units on the 205 licensed parcels the bridge could not match and the 85 matched licensed parcels whose records carry no unit count; and it is not a share of rental stock, because the assessor record does not mark which units are rented. Within those limits it is the coverage statement the data supports: licensed parcels account for 30.1 percent of the city's assessor-record units, and the share of Denver's actual long-term rental units that are licensed is not measurable from the register, the parcel spine, or the ACS totals in the warehouse.

The register's vintage, such as it is, matches the phase-in in Finding 1. This service carries no original-issue date field; the only date-like fields are EXPIRATION_DATE and RENEWAL_CREATED, and RENEWAL_CREATED reads January 1, 1900 on all 38,949 active rows (the only non-1900 values in the table are the 21 Delinquent rows), so it is a sentinel on this basis and is used for nothing here. The license number itself encodes an issue-year prefix, and on that basis the 26,941 active licenses divide as follows: 2,233 with a 2022 prefix, 13,292 with a 2023 prefix (49.3 percent), 6,203 in 2024, 3,088 in 2025, and 2,125 in 2026 to date; 91.7 percent carry a 2023 or later prefix. That distribution describes licenses active today, not the program's issuance history: surrendered and lapsed licenses from the phase-in years exit this table, so the prefix distribution is a survivor stock, not an issuance series. Its center of gravity in 2023 is consistent with the multi-unit requirement taking effect January 1, 2023 and with the four-year term: 2023 licenses remain inside their first term through 2027.

Three further field-level facts are printed because a future reader of this register will meet them. First, the table's MULTIPLE_LICENSES field reads "Stand Alone" on all 38,970 rows, including all 697 rows of the largest multi-unit license; it carries no multi-license signal, and every multi-row and multi-license figure in this brief is computed from license numbers and the parcel bridge, never from that field. Second, status and expiration disagree on a small, dated set: 2 active-status rows (0.005 percent of active rows) carry an expiration date earlier than the October 20, 2026 pull date, and the remaining 21 past-expiration rows are exactly the 21 Delinquent rows; DLCP's renewal FAQ provides a 90-day grace period after expiration during which a license can still be renewed with penalty fees, and the register alone cannot say whether any given record sits inside it, so no conclusion about any license's standing is drawn. Expiration years for active rows run 2026 to 2031, with the largest cohort expiring in 2027 (18,095 rows), the shape a four-year term produces from a 2023-centered stock. Third, the register contains 6 exact duplicate filings (a license, parcel, and address combination appearing twice; 12 rows, 6 excess, 0.02 percent of active rows), counted as published; removing them changes no figure in this brief at its printed precision. Separately, the one double-filed license from Finding 1 files 86 addresses under each of two parcel numbers; if the second filing of each address is a clerical duplication rather than a two-parcel property, that license's true row count is 86, and the register's active row total would fall by 86 (0.2 percent) - no printed figure changes at its printed precision either way, and the register itself does not say which reading is correct. ZIP is 0 on 3,436 rows and null on 773 and is used for no figure here.

## Data notes: what this register is and is not

- The published table is named "active licenses." Its contents are close to that name: 99.95 percent of its rows carry an active status, and the 21 Delinquent rows are the whole remainder. Every figure in this brief names its status basis.
- The register's unit of count is a filing row, not a license and not reliably a unit (Finding 1). Every figure in this brief names its basis: rows, distinct license numbers, holders, parcels, or assessor-record units.
- The register measures licenses, not occupancy or activity. It does not record whether a licensed unit is currently rented, vacant, or owner-occupied between tenancies, and an unlicensed rental leaves no row here at all.
- Holder identity is the ENTITY_NAME field as filed. The concentration results are statements about that field, tested against name-token merging in Finding 2. Household-level or owner-level common control exercised through distinct filed names cannot be seen in this field, and this brief does not estimate it.
- 522 active rows (1.3 percent) carry no neighborhood assignment and are excluded from parcel and neighborhood tables only; they are counted in every citywide total. Of the 522, 499 sit on parcels absent from the parcel spine and 23 sit on 22 parcels that are in the spine but carry no neighborhood value. The unassigned rows concentrate on 205 parcels, two of which hold 157 rows, so the exclusion is concentrated rather than diffuse.
- The register is republished continuously (the service's last edit before this pull was the same morning, October 20, 2026). Counts in this brief describe the October 20, 2026 snapshot, archived in the warehouse; the live count recorded on October 19, 2026 was 38,981 rows against 38,970 at this pull, a one-day change of 11 rows on a moving table.

## What this might mean

This section is interpretation; the findings above are the measurements.

Read together, the two Denver rental registers describe two differently shaped licensed markets administered by the same department: a short-term market the program's primary-residence rules force into one license per person (2,083 licenses, 2,080 holders, no business names, per the October 19, 2026 brief), and a long-term market in which a license is a property instrument that can cover one unit or several hundred (26,941 licenses, 20,780 holders, 37.1 percent of licenses under business-entity names). Within the long-term register, concentration on the license basis is moderate and measurable - most licenses are single-license holdings, the largest holder holds about one license in fifty, and holders of 11 or more licenses hold about one license in twelve - while the unit-level exposure behind those licenses cannot be ranked from the register because filing grain varies by filer. The strongest alternative explanation for the moderate license-basis concentration is that the name field understates common control: properties held through separate entities with distinct filed names, or managed under one operator for many owners, would read as dispersed here while operating as one portfolio, and the register contains no field that could settle the question. A second alternative runs the other way: some large row counts that look like concentration are a single building's units under one license, which is a statement about building size, not about how many properties a holder controls. The data in this package cannot distinguish those worlds; they differ in ownership records the register does not publish. What the register does settle is narrower: the licensed long-term footprint is citywide (all 78 neighborhoods), its license stock dates overwhelmingly from the 2023 phase-in year forward, and any statement about Denver's licensed rental market that does not name its basis - rows, licenses, or units - is ambiguous by a factor of up to 18.7 against the short-term comparison and by the full row-to-license ratio of 1.45 inside this register alone.

## Who can act

- The Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection (DLCP), which administers the residential rental licensing program and publishes this register. Two low-cost operational items follow from Findings 1 and 5: publish a grain note with the dataset stating that one row is a unit or address filing and that rows per license vary by property type, with the distinct-license count published beside the row count; and document the RENEWAL_CREATED field, which reads January 1, 1900 on every active row, so a reader of the table knows whether it is a placeholder. Cost class: low-cost operational change (staff time inside the existing open-data program). How we would know it worked: the dataset page carries the grain note and the field documentation, checkable by re-running this brief's counts against the published definitions.
- Denver City Council, during the 2027 budget deliberation now open (public hearing October 26; amendment vote November 2; final passage November 9, the charter deadline). The decision this brief informs is what the licensing system can and cannot evidence in its fourth year: the register, as published, supports holder, parcel, and neighborhood accounting of the licensed market (Findings 2 to 4), and it cannot evidence the unlicensed market or a licensed share of rental stock at all (Finding 5). Any appropriation aimed at unlicensed rentals needs a denominator source this register does not contain, such as a renter-occupied unit layer or a utility and assessor based rental estimate; funding the register's upkeep and funding unlicensed-market measurement are different lines. Cost class: budget decision. How we would know it worked: a published, dated count or estimate of the unlicensed segment from whatever source the city adopts, comparable against this brief's licensed baseline of 26,941 active licenses on 38,949 active rows.
- Neighborhood organizations in the high-density neighborhoods (Whittier, Clayton, City Park West, University, Cole). The rates in Finding 4 are per housing unit on the row basis; the organizations working in those neighborhoods hold the building-level knowledge (which properties are rentals, which are owner-occupied) that a filing rate cannot supply, and the building pattern in Finding 3 (large properties licensed as one instrument, condominiums licensed unit by unit on shared parcels) is most visible at that scale. Cost class: none. How we would know it worked: the same panel, republished with each register refresh, shows whether neighborhood row rates and license counts move.

## Methods

- Dataset: ODC_residential_rental_property_active_licenses (Denver Open Data Catalog, ArcGIS FeatureServer table 52), full-service pull October 20, 2026 (38,970 records), archived as the warehouse snapshot for that date with its pull log. The pull is asserted equal to the live service count at pull time.
- Active basis: LICENSE_STATUS equal to "License Issued - Active" exactly. The full status counter was recorded before any filter was applied (Active 38,949 rows; Delinquent 21) and sums to the 38,970 pulled records. LICENSE_TYPE is "Residential Rental Property" on every record.
- Three labeled bases: the LICENSE basis counts distinct LICENSE_NUM values; the ROW basis counts register rows; the UNIT basis, used only in Finding 5, sums TOT_UNITS from assessor parcel records. No figure mixes bases; shares are computed within one basis at a time.
- Holder identity: ENTITY_NAME normalized (uppercase, whitespace collapsed). Concentration is computed on the normalized string over the 26,939 active licenses and 38,947 active rows that carry a holder name; the 2 active rows with no ENTITY_NAME are reported in Finding 2 and excluded from holder counts only. Two merge sensitivities are printed in Finding 2 (first-and-last-token merge; first-initial-and-final-token over-merge).
- Name classification: tokens are the alphabetic words of the normalized name. Business-entity name if any token is in the documented business token set (the same 60-token set as the October 19, 2026 STR brief: LLC, INC, LTD, CORP, CORPORATION, LLP, LLLP, LP, PC, PLLC, COMPANY, CO, TRUST, HOLDINGS, PROPERTIES, REALTY, VENTURES, PARTNERS, GROUP, CAPITAL, INVESTMENTS, ENTERPRISES, MANAGEMENT, ESTATES, HOMES, RENTALS, FOUNDATION, ASSOCIATION, BANK, CHURCH, UNIVERSITY, and related tokens listed in the build code). Individual name if the name has two to five alphabetic tokens and no business token. Not determined otherwise. Shares are computed on all active licenses (and separately on rows and holders), with determined and not-determined shares summing to 100 percent.
- Parcel bridge: PARCEL_NUM is a Double in the source service (values of 10 to 12 digits, leading zeros dropped); the canonical key is str(int(PARCEL_NUM)) zero-padded to 13 characters, matched directly against parcel spine v6 (built October 15, 2026). Match rate: 38,471 of 38,970 rows register-wide (98.7 percent, by parcel number) and 38,427 of 38,949 active rows (98.7 percent) assigned a neighborhood. The active-row bridge counts a row as matched when the spine assigns it a neighborhood: by parcel number alone 38,450 active rows match, and 23 of the remaining rows sit on 22 spine parcels whose neighborhood value is null, so those rows carry no neighborhood and sit outside the parcel and neighborhood tables. Neighborhood is the spine v6 neighborhood of the assigned parcel (the city's 78 statistical neighborhoods).
- Rates: active rows per 1,000 housing units, labeled row rates. Denominators are ACS 2020-2024 5-year estimates (table B25001) at tract level for Denver County (359,581 units), allocated to statistical neighborhoods in proportion to tract area inside each neighborhood polygon (359,351 units allocated), the same denominators as the October 17, 18, and 19 briefs. The median neighborhood rate is computed across all 78 neighborhoods; this register has no zero-row neighborhoods.
- STR comparison: figures are taken from the companion October 19, 2026 STR package (stats.json and its neighborhood panel): 2,083 active licenses, citywide 5.8 licenses per 1,000 units, 75 of 78 neighborhoods. Rank correlations are Spearman coefficients across the 78 neighborhoods computed on the two panels.
- Layering admission test, applied per the brief-writing standards: the ACS housing layer is used because it converts raw neighborhood counts into exposure rates (Finding 4 could not rank neighborhoods without it); the parcel spine is used because it assigns each row a parcel and neighborhood, supplies the assessor unit counts behind the grain test in Finding 1 and the coverage figure in Finding 5, and exposes the parcel patterns in Finding 3, none of which can be stated from the register alone.
- Verification: a pre-flight audit script (Section 10 gates) ships in this package's code folder and its output is recorded in the verification file, including two built-in controls: no license number mixes statuses (each license is wholly active or wholly delinquent), and 11 of the 12 licenses with 100 or more rows sit under one holder name on one parcel with all-distinct addresses, the 12th being the documented cross-parcel double filing described in Finding 1. Every figure in this brief is cross-checked against the package stats file.
